What is real and what is fitted. The front propagation is exact geometry — an arbitrary ignition evolves into a fire shape, separate fronts merge, the perimeter wraps an obstacle, none of it prescribed. Fuel consumption and Byram fireline intensity are conservation statements. The local spread rate is a regression fitted to 1980s experimental fires at Annaburroo, N.T., and the flame length is a separate correlation. So the fire's organisation is modelled; its speed is measured.

Fire shape is a modelling choice. Cheney 1993 reports a head-fire rate of spread only — its own “fire shape” variable is a categorical head-fire shape (pointed vs parabolic), used as a regression covariate. The paper contains no length-to-breadth or elliptical relation, so turning one number into a 2D front needs an assumption it does not supply. Wind-projected evaluates the fitted law at the wind component along the front normal — no new constants, but it extends the law to directions it was never fitted against. Isotropic is the assumption-free baseline and is visibly wrong in wind.

Two spread models. Hybrid is the parent project's validated Phase 20 “Option B” blend: below U₂ ≈ 1.8 m/s the rate is Cheney's regression, above U₂ ≈ 2.5 it is a full 3D solver (k-ε RANS + EDC combustion + DOM radiation + level-set front), with a linear ramp between. That threshold is not a tuning knob — it is where the resolved closure stops propagating correctly, because low-wind spread is mediated by intermittent flame contact that a RANS average removes (Cheney 1998 §3.2; Finney 2015, PNAS 112:9833). Phase 19 put the threshold at 1.4 m/s and left a hole: natural grass at 4% moisture and U₁₀ = 2 resolved to 6.56 m/min against Cheney's 26.42. Option B raises it above that hole and scores 19/20 on the project's [⅓, 3] acceptance band. Fit only disables the solver side, so you can see what it contributes.

The solver is not running here. One case takes ten minutes to an hour, so the applet carries precomputed results and interpolates them — the solver's answers, faithfully, not the solver. They are stored as a correction ratio to Cheney (0.58–0.94 across the sweep) rather than as rates, because a near-flat ratio interpolates safely off a few samples where a steep power law does not. The solver was run at 4% and 8% moisture only, so between those it interpolates and outside them it holds the nearest value rather than extrapolating a two-point trend.

The side view is derived, not drawn. Flame length is Byram's L_f = 0.0775·I_B^0.46; the tilt is Byram's convective Froude relation tan θ = 0.88·√(U_mf²/gL_f), so the flame lies over as wind rises and its vertical reach H = L_f·cos θ falls even at constant flame length. The depth of the flaming zone is ROS × residence time, read out of the arrival-time field rather than assumed. Bed depth is Cheney's Table 3 measurement.

The numbers are exact; the picture is a few percent slow. Every figure in the readout and the position of the marker on the plot are evaluated straight from the law, so they carry no numerical error. The animation is a level set on a 1 m grid, and first-order upwinding lags a curved front by an amount proportional to cell size — about 6% for a point ignition here, falling to 3.4% at 0.5 m and 1.8% at 0.25 m. A straight front is reproduced exactly. The grid stays at 1 m because halving it costs eight times the work per frame, and correcting the discrepancy by nudging the speed would stop the model from solving the equation it claims to.

Cheney NP, Gould JS, Catchpole WR (1993). “The influence of fuel, weather and fire shape variables on fire-spread in grasslands.” International Journal of Wildland Fire 3(1): 31–44. Fireline intensity and flame length after Byram (1959); residence time after Anderson (1969). Wind is the 2 m reference throughout, which is the paper's own variable.
